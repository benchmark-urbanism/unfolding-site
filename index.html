Unfolding Urbanism
OverviewTruchetGirihPenroseAutomataSolariJacquardPassacagliaFugue

A city is a web of exchange, social, economic, and of every other kind, more complex than we can describe or predict. When people have acted on it with intent, they have often damaged its fabric, especially at the pedestrian scale, and left it less able to carry the rich potential of interactions that unfold as a process and cannot be optimised as a static solution in advance. The question becomes how to act with intent while preserving this rich and complex fabric and its future unfolding, both at the pedestrian scale and at the wider, emergent scale.

The city as process

The answer history offers is a pattern, modular and iterable, simple enough for anyone to use and cohesive enough not to interfere with the city’s wider dynamics.

Cities are accretions of process, knitted together as they unfold across space and time, congealing and dissolving by turns. Unfolding urbanism works with that rhythm.

The pages that follow trace that idea through art, architecture, music, and towns, where such patterns have carried rich and emergent complexity.

Its elements are modular patterns, designed for the pedestrian scale. Small and composable, they let a designer intervene locally, with a light touch. Used incrementally, they allow intervention to proceed one street or square at a time, answering to the larger unfolding of the city as it goes.

The simplicity is deceptive. Because the approach is modular and incremental, it can respond as the rhythms of the city rise and fall. Heavier-handed approaches interfere with those rhythms. They are brittle for that reason, and costly, and when they fail the damage can be extensive and can last for generations.

The unfolding city celebrates process, not automation. It values human engagement over decisions driven by algorithm, and uses technology in its service. It relies on the city’s own intelligence, which rests on feedback and small steps, and which cannot be replaced by an external or automated process.

Static urbanism

Unfolding urbanism is the antithesis of the static master plan.

After the Second World War, the car, the utility network, and mass production made it possible to build at a new scale, and planners set out to solve the city in one stroke, as though it were a problem with an answer. What they missed was that a city is itself a process. It has no final form, and it asks for vision tempered by the capacity for fine-grained change, a direction held over time and open to what each step reveals. Its streets had been its connective tissue, a public realm open to anyone, where shops, doorways, and passers-by met in one fine mesh. The new plans inverted that. The street became a conduit for through traffic, uses were zoned apart, and housing went into towers on open ground or out to the suburbs, a scatter of private enclaves in lieu of a public realm. In trying to solve the city, planners took away much of its capacity for process, and the mistake recurs when a new technology is used to shortcut that process instead of serving it.

A process needs grain in space and in time. A city that changes one plot or one street at a time gives the public a way in at each step, and it gives the authorities changes small enough to fund and to trial before anything is taken further. Master plans typically lack this grain, in their spatial layout and in their ownership alike, and so they are hard to alter once built, and that rigidity can damage the wider fabric of the city.

Current development practice compounds the problem. Clients want a predictable budget, a firm end date, and a concrete deliverable, and the master plan is the usual vehicle for them. The unfolding city answers with modular patterns, set out as an incremental playbook. They give a client units that can be seen and costed in advance, and because they are laid one at a time, each returns feedback as the work proceeds while the place stays open to change. Savannah’s wards and Cerdà’s grid for Barcelona were frameworks of this kind, drawn with intention and filled in over a century.

The design challenge

How do we hold a vision and act on it with determination, while leaving room for spontaneity and for the dynamics that emerge as a city unfolds?

Artists, scientists, architects, and composers have each met a version of this problem in the same way, with a modular substrate and a toolkit of interoperable parts that allow for intentioned local interventions while remaining cohesive with the wider fabric and its emergent processes.

Precedent · A city is not a tree

Christopher Alexander spent his career on this question.

In “A City Is Not a Tree” (1965) he showed what goes wrong when a city is planned for visual order and efficiency at the large scale, and its connections at the small scale are overlooked. Such an order imposes a rigid and fragmented spatial hierarchy, which limits the ways in which spaces can connect to one another. In that hierarchy each part is kept in its own compartment. A living city lets its parts cross-connect, so that a corner shop is a stop on the walk to work, a meeting place for its street, a living for the family behind the counter, and a landmark for a stranger. His answer came in A Pattern Language (1977), a substrate of 253 patterns from the region to the room, each a recurring problem with a way of solving it, made to be combined by whoever builds. A New Theory of Urban Design (1987) turned to the process itself, a city grown through a sequence of projects, each judged by what it adds to the whole already there.

The problem · The mind can hold only so much

Alexander traced the tree to a limit of the mind.

In 1871 William Stanley Jevons threw beans into a box and guessed each count at a glance, and he was reliably right up to four. Reviewing the evidence on working memory in 2001, Nelson Cowan put its span at about four items. A tree suits a mind of that size, since each group can be taken in on its own and the whole grasped a level at a time. A semilattice, Alexander’s name for the overlapping web, cannot be read that way. Its overlaps multiply far faster than its parts, and in a city most of them lie out of sight, in relations between people and places that differ by the street and by the hour. A designer who sets out to settle the city in one pass cannot know them, and so faces a choice. One course, which Alexander found in his natural cities, is a substrate in which the combinations of parts stay open, to be remade as each person, day, and circumstance requires. The other reduces those relations to a few assumptions and fixes them in advance, as a composition on the drawing board, a network modelled on the utilities, or a street pattern prepared by a traffic engineer, and holds the city to the one arrangement it was drawn with.

Precedent · Pace layers

A substrate holds the local and the global in balance by letting them change at different speeds and keeping the speeds in touch.

Stewart Brand, in How Buildings Learn (1994), took from the architect Frank Duffy the observation that a building is layers that change at different rates (its site, structure, skin, services, space plan, and stuff), and in 1999 he widened it to a civilisation: fashion, commerce, infrastructure, governance, culture, and nature, from the fastest to the slowest. On a street the layers are the walker’s minute, the shop’s month, the owner’s year, the street’s decades, and the ground’s centuries. The layers hold each other in check by feedback. The slow set the bounds, the fast try changes within them, and what proves itself there settles over time into the slower layers. The cross-connections Alexander found in a living city are not the feedback itself. They are what permit it. Where a corner shop is tied into several overlapping uses, each can change without the others having to, so the fast layers evolve instead of being locked in place by the slow.

Fast learns, slow remembers. Fast proposes, slow disposes. Fast is discontinuous, slow is continuous. Fast and small instructs slow and big by accrued innovation and by occasional revolution. Slow and big controls small and fast by constraint and constancy. Fast gets all our attention, slow has all the power.Stewart Brand, 2018

The problem · No substitute for process

It is widely assumed that a city can be engineered and optimised in one pass, if only the design is good enough.

The assumption is mistaken, because what a set of rules will do cannot in general be read off from the rules. In the 1930s Kurt Gödel showed that any consistent set of rules rich enough for arithmetic leaves some of its own questions unsettled, and Alan Turing that no general method can tell in advance whether a procedure will ever halt. The rows of black and white cells beside this text, the ones Stephen Wolfram studied, show the same limit on a small scale. Each cell takes its colour from itself and its two neighbours by one fixed rule, and yet for rule 110, as far as anyone knows, the thousandth row can be reached only by working out every row before it. Wolfram called this computational irreducibility. There is no shortcut for process. A city is far richer than these cells, and it has no last row. It is not a single end state to be optimised. This does not mean that intentional interventions and design are not possible, only that it matters whether that design leaves open a substrate within which processes are free to unfold. The city itself is by nature a process, an unfolding that trades information day by day between the slow and the fast, the local and the global. Whatever a new technology promises, it cannot stand in for that unfolding. It can take a part in the design at the pedestrian scale, and in the feedback between the layers, provided it keeps that feedback intact.

The problem · Organised complexity

In “Science and Complexity” (1948) Warren Weaver sorted the problems of science into three kinds.

Problems of simplicity, such as two balls on a billiard table, yield to an equation. Problems of disorganised complexity, a table with millions of balls colliding at random, yield to averages, the methods of statistical mechanics. Between them lie problems of organised complexity, which deal with a sizeable number of factors “interrelated into an organic whole”. Jane Jacobs argued in 1961 that a city is a problem of this third kind, in which dozens of quantities vary “simultaneously and in subtly interconnected ways”. A city holds intention and existing order alongside the order that arises spontaneously from the interaction of its parts, and as each shapes the other, the city unfolds in ways that are hard to predict. Planning framed in the other two terms proceeded much as Jacobs described, and damaged the city’s capacity for feedback and spontaneous adaptation.

That loss mattered because feedback is where a city’s intelligence lies. From the cross-talk between its parts, a higher-order intelligence emerges of its own accord, able to solve hard problems and to adapt to constraints and opportunities that were not foreseen. It can do harm as well, as when rising rents drive out the people who made a street busy, and in either case a plan fixed in advance cannot keep pace. W. Ross Ashby’s law of requisite variety (1956) holds that a system can be steered only by responses as varied as the disturbances it meets, and in a city that variety comes from many people, each acting locally on a shared substrate. A slime mould shows the same capacity without a brain. In 2010 Atsushi Tero and colleagues placed one among oat flakes laid out as the towns around Tokyo, and it grew a network close to the region’s railways in efficiency and in tolerance of breaks.

Complexity through simplicity

Each of the cases that follow is a substrate of this kind.

In each a framework holds while a toolkit of simple parts acts within it. Local action stays within reach, the whole coheres by the same means, and information passes between the scales. The heading over each names what that interplay supports there: flexibility, a whole by design, variety from simplicity, emergent computation, simple by design, complexity programmed, a toolkit of parts, and the human in the loop. Most are stated first in mathematics, craft, or music, and then found in a town. Many of the figures are live, and eight open as pages of their own, where the patterns can be tried by hand.

Flexibility · Truchet: one tile

Truchet’s system begins with one square tile, cut on its diagonal, with one half dark.

In 1704 Sébastien Truchet, a priest and engineer, showed that its four orientations yield an unlimited number of designs. A board of 144 tiles admits 4 to the power 144 arrangements, far more than could be laid out or inspected, and a choice made at each tile throws up structure at scales well above it. The tile does not change, so the variety lies in the arrangement. There is no rule between neighbours. Any tile can be turned at any time, and the design above it shifts with the turn. So simple a system bends to almost any design, and with nothing to steer it, what it makes lies close to chance.

Flexibility · In towns · The medina: rules between neighbours

The medina of Tunis is the same flexibility at the scale of a town.

Thousands of households built it over centuries, without a plan, under a handful of rules between neighbours, which Besim Hakim recovered from Maliki legal texts and court cases. A property may be used as its owner wishes, provided no harm is done to others. No window may be placed so that it looks into a neighbour’s house. A public street must be wide enough for two loaded camels to pass.

Most of the rules set no form, and the one that does sets a minimum, so building stayed within each household’s reach and free in form, and the street stayed public. The rules were applied case by case as disputes arose, each decision joining what the next was judged against. The plan of the medina records those decisions.

The whole by design · Girih tiles

The walls of the Darb-i Imam shrine in Isfahan (1453) and the Gunbad-i Kabud tomb tower in Maragha (1197), in Iran, bear girih, the Persian word for knot and the name for the interlaced strapwork of Islamic architecture.

In 2007 the physicists Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt showed in Science that many such surfaces could be laid from five tiles, each scored with lines that join at the edges into stars and bands spanning several tiles. The Topkapı Scroll, a Timurid pattern book of the late fifteenth century, shows the outlines of the tiles beneath the strapwork of several panels, evidence that the artisans worked with them. Lu thought the tiles might have been “just a way for artisans to construct their art more easily”.

Truchet’s tile may sit in any orientation beside any other. The girih adds one rule at the edge, that the corners close and the lines run on. It is checked only where two tiles meet, so an artisan needs no compass work and no drawing of the whole wall to add to it, and lays each tile by hand where it fits. Yet the whole is not left to chance. The stars and bands are cut into the tiles, and the edge rule carries them from tile to tile across the wall.

The whole by design · In towns · Savannah: the ward

Savannah’s surveyors worked from a single drawing, the ward, which James Oglethorpe, founder of the colony of Georgia, laid out in 1733 for a colonial town on Yamacraw land.

Each ward is a square with four blocks of ten house lots and four trust lots for public buildings around it, and Oglethorpe set the wards within a regional framework of garden and farm lots. As with the girih, the difficult thinking was done once, within the module, which carries the relations of the whole with it. Wherever a ward was laid, each house lot fell within about a block of a square and the streets lined up with the next ward’s, so the town could be extended by repetition with no drawing of the finished town. One survey party could stake out a ward, and each new ward was a complete neighbourhood. Savannah grew in this way from four wards in 1733 to twenty-four by 1851. When the city later spread south on a plain grid, the squares stopped where the ward was set aside.

Variety from simplicity · Penrose tiles

A set of tiles that can cover the plane only without repeating is called aperiodic, and the number of shapes such a set needs was reduced in stages.

In 1966 Robert Berger, in proving that no general procedure can decide whether a given set of tiles will cover the plane, found such a set of 20,426. By 1971 Raphael Robinson had reduced it to six, and in 1974 the physicist Roger Penrose to two. In 2023 David Smith and colleagues found a single shape, the hat, that does so with its mirror image, and then the spectre, which needs no mirror. Penrose’s two rhombs carry marks on their edges that must match where tiles meet. Two shapes and one rule at the edge admit infinitely many tilings, none of which repeats. Any finite patch of one tiling occurs in every other, so the local fabric is shared, while the pattern as a whole does not repeat.

Aperiodic order also occurs in matter. In 1982 Dan Shechtman observed an alloy whose diffraction pattern showed tenfold symmetry, then held impossible in a crystal. He published the finding in 1984, and in the same year Dov Levine and Paul Steinhardt gave crystallographers a model for such order in the Penrose tiling. Such materials are now called quasicrystals. For towns the tiling also shows the limit of a rule held at the edge. A tile that fits its neighbours may leave no lawful tile further on, so a local choice is not always free, and more than the neighbours must be read before the next piece is laid.

Variety from simplicity · In towns · Settlements grown by one rule

The hamlets of the Vaucluse, in Provence, grew house by house.

Bill Hillier and Julienne Hanson, the founders of space syntax, modelled them in The Social Logic of Space (1984) with a restricted random process. Houses are added at random, each with a cell of open space in front of it, and that cell must share a side with another. Run repeatedly, the rule grows a beady ring, an open space that widens and narrows like beads on a string and stays continuous. Each house is placed freely against the open space the earlier houses left. The rule binds only the public space, so the settlement can take many shapes and still be crossed on foot as it grows. The model reproduces the spatial type of the hamlets and makes no claim about how they were built.

In a Ba-ila settlement in southern Zambia, the house, the family ring, and the village share one structure, each with a gated front and a back of higher status. Ron Eglash, in African Fractals (1999), models the plan as one rule applied three times, each time to the result of the last, so that a single relation between front and back orders household, family, and village alike.

Emergent computation · Cellular automata

Settlements grow by rules applied again and again to what is already there, and the rows of cells that Stephen Wolfram studied show such rules in a minimal form.

A cell and its two neighbours give eight cases, each answered black or white, so there are 256 rules. Some settle into uniformity, some into a repeat, and some into apparent randomness or long-lived structures. Rule 30, started from a single black cell, produces a pattern that passes tests for randomness, and rule 110, by a proof Matthew Cook published in 2004, can compute anything a computer can. Each cell sees only its neighbourhood, yet the row as a whole processes information, and the written rule does not show whether it will. By analogy, the cells are neighbours in a town, and what they compute together is the town’s capacity to adapt.

Emergent computation · In towns · The subak: a watershed coordinated by neighbours

On Bali, farmers who follow only their neighbours coordinate the water of a river system.

A subak is a group of rice farmers who share one water source, and the subaks are linked by a network of water temples that reaches up to Lake Batur. The canals and temples stay fixed, and each subak decides its own planting date. Planting together deprives pests of a host, while planting apart spreads the demand for water, and each subak can strike that balance only in relation to its neighbours. J. Stephen Lansing has studied Balinese irrigation since the 1970s. In his model of 2017, after each harvest every subak copies the planting schedule of whichever neighbour did best. No subak sees the whole watershed, and over successive harvests the yields approach the best for the whole of it.

From 1971 continuous planting was ordered instead, as part of the government’s drive to raise rice yields under the Green Revolution. By the late 1970s pests took a large part of the crop, and the order was revoked in 1988. Lansing reads the losses as the cost of replacing local adjustment with a single rule set from above, which removed the feedback between neighbours.

Simple by design · Solari: row by row

The split-flap departures board, made by the Italian firm Solari di Udine since the 1950s, is simple by design.

Each character sits on a drum of hinged flaps, and the drum turns until the right flap falls into view. A drum can do only this, yet a wall of the same drum, repeated, lists a station’s departures and changes them as the trains move. What the board says is decided by whatever drives it. The board demonstrates the power of a modular format of design. Simple parts, repeated, become a medium for information that emerges across the whole. On the Solari page the drums spell a word and then list departures.

Complexity programmed · The loom and the draft

Cloth, too, is made row by row, the weft passing across a fixed warp.

The loom that Joseph Marie Jacquard built in 1804 read which threads to lift from a punched card, one card to a row, so that the design of a cloth was held as a chain of cards, and a weaver could alter a row by changing its card. The chain of cards is a programme made of simple parts. From it came cloth too complex to hold in mind, programmed on purpose more than a century before the electronic computer. The warp is laid once, and the card is chosen row by row. A town’s streets and plots are its warp, and the uses that pass through them its weft. Charles Babbage later took the punched card for his Analytical Engine, a design for a mechanical computer, and Ada Lovelace drew the comparison.

We may say most aptly that the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves.Ada Lovelace, 1843

Anni Albers, trained in weaving at the Bauhaus, drafted her weaves first, writing the threading and the lifts on squared paper as rules she could revise before a thread was set. In her later prints she took up the triangle, and through the 1970s she filled a graph-paper notebook with such drawings. A grid square cut on its diagonal into two triangles is Truchet’s tile, and Albers chose hers square by square, judging the sheet as it accumulated.

The toolkit of parts · Bach's Passacaglia

Johann Sebastian Bach’s Passacaglia in C minor for organ shows how much variation a few small parts can yield.

A passacaglia is built on a ground, a short bass line repeated while the music above it varies. Bach’s ground has fifteen notes and is stated twenty-one times, mostly in the bass and for a stretch in the upper voices, and it holds the piece together. Over the statements, often two at a time, a new cell is explored in turn, a figure of four or five notes whose rhythm and shape are kept exact so that it can be repeated and combined. The cells are the toolkit. The piece works through what each can do on its own and what they can do together across four voices, each heard against the ground and against the cells before it. As an analogy, it shows how a modular system can be designed.

The toolkit of parts · In towns · Copenhagen: street by street

Cities can also proceed one move at a time, each judged by what the last produced.

In 1962 Copenhagen closed Strøget, its main shopping street, to cars as a trial, against the fears of its shopkeepers, and went on street by street. From 1968 Jan Gehl counted the people walking and staying in the centre, and his counts were used to judge each step. The moves were few in kind and repeated: a street closed to cars, a car park made a square, two or three per cent of the parking removed each year, and chairs and benches added where people stayed. The area given to people on foot grew from 15,800 square metres in 1962 to nearly 100,000 by 1996, the number staying on a summer afternoon grew more than threefold, and in the same years eighteen squares that had been car parks were made over.

In 1970 the University of Oregon engaged Alexander’s group to replace its master plan with the same kind of process, yearly surveys and small projects, published as The Oregon Experiment (1975). The university’s plan still states that planning follows “a process rather than an established image of the campus”.

The toolkit of parts · In towns · New York: the plaza kit

New York worked from a kit of parts.

From 2008 the city’s transport department, under its commissioner Janette Sadik-Khan, took pieces of roadway back with what could be set out in a day, paint and gravel on the asphalt, planters, and movable chairs and tables, and counted what followed. On 24 May 2009 it closed Broadway through Times Square as a trial, with folding lawn chairs bought that week, and in February 2010, with injuries to pedestrians down by a third and three in four New Yorkers surveyed saying the square had improved, the closure was made permanent. The parts were few and cheap. The arrangement was tried on the ground and changed where the counts said so, and only what worked was built in stone. Dozens of plazas came from the same kit, each arranged for its own corner.

The human in the loop · The fugue: voices co-evolving

The fugue that follows the Passacaglia without a break is the more dynamic case.

A fugue takes a single theme, its subject, and passes it through the voices. Each voice enters with the subject in turn, the others continue with countersubjects that fit above or below, and the threads cross into figures shared between the voices. Here the subject is the first half of the ground, short enough for each voice to carry. The composer places each entry in view of what the other voices are doing. Bach could do this on the spot. At Hamburg in 1720 he improvised on a chorale for close to half an hour before the aged Reincken, and the G minor organ fugue, BWV 542, is thought to date from the same visit. At Potsdam in 1747 Frederick the Great gave him a theme, and he improvised a fugue on it, later written out in the Musical Offering. The fugue is both co-evolutionary and emergent. Its lines grow out of one another, and the figures they form arise from their combination. The composer guides that emergence entry by entry, with parts that are modular and iterable, a toolkit worked with a human in the loop. A designer can work in a similar way within a street, where shops, pedestrians, landlords, and residents compete and collaborate, and the character of the street grows out of their exchanges over time.

The human in the loop · In towns · Barcelona: the superblock

In Barcelona the first superblock of the 2016 programme, on Salvador Rueda’s model, closed nine blocks in Poblenou to through traffic.

It was laid out almost overnight and met a petition of some two thousand signatures. It was made from a kit: paint on the carriageway, planters, benches, picnic tables, and play equipment, set on the asphalt and cheap enough to move. The city formed a steering group with the objectors, let a bus route through, and rearranged the kit, and only when the layout had settled were the trees planted and the streets rebuilt. Later superblocks were planned with residents from the start, each combining the same parts in its own way.

What stands now was settled in exchanges with the people it touched: residents who objected, shopkeepers who feared for their trade, the bus operator whose route had been cut, and the city, each acting on what the others had just done. The designers decided each change in view of them. Critics have since pointed to rising rents inside some superblocks.

The human in the loop · The game

In the cases above a framework held while its parts were laid, turned, or rewritten within it, and in the towns someone read what each step produced before taking the next.

The game puts the reader in that position. It sets two streets in competition for one pool of households, for visitors from outside, and for through traffic. Each street is a loop of eight blocks, programmed in the manner of a Jacquard loom, and the reader chooses each move against what the earlier ones have produced.
