Automata

A rule on three cells, run on a row of two hundred and forty.

An elementary cellular automaton is a row of cells, each on or off. Time runs downward, and each row is the row above, rewritten by one rule.

Scroll to read. The board runs as you read.

Three make one

A cell's next state depends on three cells: itself and its two neighbours. Three cells, each on or off, give eight possible cases. All the cells are updated together.

Eight cases, one answer each

Each case can have one of two answers, so there are 256 possible rules. A rule is numbered by its answers read as binary. This is rule 110.

Click an answer to change it, and the board runs the new rule. Stephen Wolfram explored all 256 in A New Kind of Science (2002).

The rule's number can also be typed beneath the board.

Local rules, and what they make

Each rule here is local and deterministic. A cell's next state follows from three cells by a table of eight entries, so one cell's next step is easy to predict.

Across a whole row, some rules produce structures at a larger scale. These move along the row, meet, and carry information, and they are found by running the rule.

Which rules matter

Most of the 256 give uniformity or a repeat. Rule 250 fills the row. Rule 184 is traffic: each black cell is a car that moves on when the cell ahead is empty, and above half density jams form that move backwards while the cars move forwards. In 2008 Yuki Sugiyama and colleagues put twenty-two cars on a circular track and the same jam formed, with no bottleneck and no accident, from small differences in how each driver followed the car ahead. Some give chaos. Rule 30 makes an apparently random pattern from one cell, and Mathematica drew its random numbers from its centre column for years. Others give structures that move, meet, and pass information. Rule 110 is one, and its collisions can run any program (Cook, 2004).

Signals

Rule 110 on its own background, dimmed here so the gliders show. Some stand still, and some run right or left. The outcome depends on which gliders meet and how. Two gliders vanish, two leave a standing one, and a standing one that is struck becomes a walker or bursts in two. Matthew Cook's proof, published in 2004, shows that arrangements of these collisions can run any program.

The supervisor

The supervisor is an algorithm that leaves the rules as they are. It chooses which rule fires when, seeds the row, and reads the result.

Here it adds two numbers. They sit in binary on one row, A in the grey cells and B in the ink cells to their right, least significant at the right. Rule 60 makes every cell the XOR of itself and its left neighbour, so one row of it writes the sum bits into B's cells. Rule 192 makes every cell the AND of the two, so one row of it writes the carries. The supervisor fires 60, fires 192, moves the carries one place left, seeds the row again, and repeats until no carry is left.

Change the numbers beneath the board, up to 99 each.

A vote

A ring of 240 cells, black or white at random. No rule of this kind finds the majority colour correctly for every starting row (Land and Belew, 1995). Two rules in turn can. Rule 184 runs for half the ring, and spreads the minority out until no two of its cells touch. Then rule 232, the majority of three, fills what is left (Fukś, 1997). The supervisor makes one decision, when to switch, and the row settles to the majority colour.

Clear deals a fresh ring.

Measures

Measure counts three things over the rows on the board. The first is how many of the 256 patterns of eight cells appear. The second is how many bits a row takes to describe. The third is the share of rows that are new.

For comparison, rule 184 gives 225 patterns, 36 bits, and 6% new. Rule 110 gives 233, 145, and 42%. Rule 30 gives 256, 260, and 73%.
