Girih

Five tiles and one rule for how they fit.

A decagon, a pentagon, a hexagon, a bow tie, and a rhombus. Every edge is the same length, so any tile can meet any other. Builders in Iran and Central Asia used them from about 1200.

Scroll to read. Click the board at any point.

The lines

Each tile carries lines that cross its edges at 54°, so they run straight on from tile to tile. A finished wall shows only the lines. The tiles are the layout underneath, and the stars and bands are what the lines make where the tiles meet.

Tiles and Lines, beneath the board, show or hide each.

The rule

At every corner the angles add up to 360°. Two decagons and a rhombus close: 144, 144, and 72. A decagon, a pentagon, and a rhombus leave 36°. No tile has a 36° corner, so that gap is a hole, shown red.

Laying tiles

Pick a tile beneath the board. Its ghost follows the pointer, snaps to the nearest open edge, and lies towards the pointer. Click to place it. If the tile does not fit, a grey ghost offers one that does.

An edge that no tile can meet is stranded. A placement that would leave a hole or strand an edge shows red and is refused. Remove takes a tile out.

Drag to move the board. Pinch, or hold ctrl and turn the wheel, to zoom. Fit shows the whole.

On a touch screen, tap once to see the ghost, and tap the ghost to place it. Remove works the same way.

Over a larger space

Two hundred tiles, grown from one decagon by the rule. The rosette is not drawn. It appears where the lines of neighbouring tiles cross. Lay the same five tiles again, choosing differently, and the wall comes out different, with the same kind of order in it.

Grow lays twelve tiles at a time by the rule, closing the most corners it can. Symmetry repeats each placement fivefold about the first tile.

Complexity for little effort

Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt found that by about 1200 the patterns had been “reconceived as tessellations of a special set of equilateral polygons” with lines drawn on them, and that “these tiles enabled the creation of increasingly complex periodic girih patterns”. By the fifteenth century, with self-similar subdivision, the tiles gave “nearly perfect quasi-crystalline Penrose patterns, five centuries before their discovery in the West”.

The tiles were a tool kit “for generating huge numbers of distinctive tile patterns without the lengthy, painstaking, and often flawed process of creating each line segment individually”. In Lu's words they may have been “just a way for artisans to construct their art more easily”. A builder could follow the rule tile by tile and arrive at a complex pattern.

The board shows a wall grown from one decagon without symmetry.

Localised order without a plan

The rule is permissive. At each placement the craftsman chooses which tile to lay and which way round. Others can pitch in around the edges, piecemeal and incrementally, without an overarching plan, and still conform to the whole, because each choice keeps to the rule that lets the next tile be laid.

A kit of parts lets each person make choices of their own that still fit with the work of others. Walls were laid this way at Isfahan, and some cities have grown in a similar way.

Maragha, Topkapi, Isfahan

The Gunbad-i Kabud tomb tower at Maragha, 1197, is one of the earliest known girih walls. Its decoration looks like curves and knots. Underneath it are girih tiles, each carrying its own piece of the pattern.

The Topkapi Scroll, a craftsman's pattern book of the fifteenth century, thirty metres long, draws small girih tiles in black and, in red over them, the same tiles at a larger scale, which the small ones fill. The Darb-i Imam shrine at Isfahan, 1453, carries two such patterns at two scales on one spandrel. In 2007 Lu and Steinhardt mapped its tiles onto Penrose tiles and found 11 mismatches in more than 3,700.
