Fugue

One subject, two countersubjects, and four voices.

A fugue begins with one voice playing a theme, its subject. A second voice then takes up the subject at another pitch, as the answer, while the first goes on to a line of its own. A third voice enters, then a fourth. After that the subject returns in one voice or another, and the other voices play against it. Here the subject is the first half of the Passacaglia’s ground, and the fugue follows the Passacaglia without a break.

Scroll to read. The button under the wheel plays.

The subject and its companions

Two other lines travel with the subject, the countersubjects, one in blue and one in green. When a voice takes up the subject, other voices take up the countersubjects above or below it, and across the twelve entries the three are dealt to the voices in changing combinations. On the wheel the subject is in ink, and the entries of the three stand on the outer ring.

Between the entries

In the episodes between entries, Bach works with fragments of the themes. A figure may be repeated a step higher, or taken up by another voice a beat later. The fragments join into lines that pass between voices, and the harmony forms from the voices sounding together. The lines and the harmony are emergent, and Bach wrote each note of them. He could do the same at the keyboard, improvising a fugue on a theme handed to him.

Reading the page

The wheel carries the fugue once round and turns under the red line, with the notes sounding at the line and pitch rising outwards. Its hub shows the harmony, the chord of the moment drawn on a dial of the twelve notes. The dashboard shows the three themes and how often each has been heard, whole or in pieces, the twelve bars now passing, and four readings of the whole fugue.

The page follows the playing, with the wheel and the dashboard together.
